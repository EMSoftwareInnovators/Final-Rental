Sunset Video, 4412 Delaney Ave. October 1996. Nine at night until midnight, and you are the only one on.

Tapes come back. Some are rewound, some are not, some are days overdue and the person handing them over has a story about why. You take the late fees, you run the unwound ones through the rewinder, and you put every tape back on its own genre run. Other people pull tapes off the shelves themselves and take their time about it, and they have opinions about your carpet, your horror section, and how long you are taking.

Part-way through the shift a county deputy comes in and reads you a description of a man who has been killing people who work counters after ten at night.

Some nights he comes in. He is polite. He pays cash. He asks what time you lock up, and whether there is anyone in the back.

The job is a real job

  • Money is paper. Nothing goes straight into the register. They hand you a bill, it sits in your hand until you walk it over and ring it up, and if they gave you a twenty for a three dollar rental they are standing at your counter until you count their change back out of the drawer.
  • Late fees are a dollar a day, two on a game. Not everyone agrees they are late. Some of them are lying and some of them are right, and you cannot tell which from the screen.
  • Seven runs to shelve. A tape on the wrong one counts against your shift. So does one you shelved without rewinding, and so does anything still lying about at closing.
  • The line is a line. Whoever reaches it first is first. Everyone has a fuse and the length of it depends on who they are, and somebody who runs out of patience walks out.
  • Midnight is a lock on the door, not a bell. Nobody else comes in. Everybody already inside still gets to pick something out and pay for it.

And the people are the job

Some of them are in the right store with the wrong idea entirely: the tape that has to work in a Betamax, the one returned to the wrong chain, the man who has wheeled his entire VCR in on a cart because yours does not make the noise. Some of them are not all there at all — a man wants the number three with no onions and asks whether the shake machine is working. You can explain, or you can play along and see how far it goes. Playing along makes them love you, and people who love you buy things.

Then there are the seventeen regulars nobody wants. They are not transactions, they are situations, and most of them cost you real minutes:

  • The man who brings his own music, sets a boombox down on your floor and turns it up. Nobody comes to the counter while it is playing, and he has to walk back to it, switch it off and carry it out.
  • The smell, and the man at the television, who cannot be told and have to be worn down over a lot of conversations — and the room still smells of him for a while after the door shuts.
  • A sovereign citizen with a folder, reading you statutes that do not exist, for as long as you let him.
  • A man contacted in his sleep by people who do not use telephones, who needs to get into a basement this store has never had. Refusing does not work. An address for the other Sunset, across town, does.
  • A woman who wants your manager. Talking is a wall. The regional manager is forty minutes away and asleep, and the phone cord only reaches the counter.
  • A man who calls first and orders a pizza, then turns up to collect it. The only thing that ends it is a real pizza, on your counter, with his toppings on it.
  • A man who gets behind your counter and empties the popcorn tub into the kettle — and then it is on your floor, and the vacuum is in the back room.

And once in a while a coach comes off the highway and three or four dozen people get out of it, every one of them dressed exactly the same, because they are all going to the same thing. They come through the door together and they all want a movie. About one in four wants to tell you about the journey first. He does not work a night the coach comes.

The description is all you get

The deputy tells you what they have, and it grows: three things to check on the first night, ten by the end of a long run. That is harder, not easier. A short list clears somebody fast. A long one means every ordinary customer matches four or five of it, and the question stops being does he match and becomes does he match all of it — which takes time you do not have with three people in line.

Everything the deputy can describe is really on the model. A ponytail is a ponytail, a limp is a limp, the duffel bag is there. Standing in front of somebody takes in the obvious things; getting close tells you what they smell like; talking tells you what they sound like. TAB holds the bulletin against whoever is in front of you, with the matches marked. Somebody can match four of six lines and be nobody.

He may come in first as a customer, browse, and check out a tape. He is the calmest person who will talk to you all night, and he will ask you questions. How you answer them changes how long you have later.

If you are sure: lock the front door, then pick up the phone. Dispatch reads back everyone in the store, each described so no two of them read alike. The deadbolt only buys minutes. Locking it is not the win — the phone is.

If you are wrong: they put a regular customer face down on the carpet in front of six people, and the district manager drives in at two in the morning to take your keys.

Four ways a night ends. He never shows and you clock out into a harder shift tomorrow. He gets in and reaches you. You call it in on the wrong person. Or you call it in on the right one.

There is also CASUAL SHIFT, which is just the store. Nobody is coming for you.

Made out of nothing

No engine, no libraries, and not one asset file. Every polygon, texture, animation and sound in this game is generated at runtime by about 17,000 lines of JavaScript.

The renderer is a software triangle rasterizer written to reproduce the hardware of a classic PlayStation rather than imitate it with a filter — integer vertex snapping for the polygon wobble, affine texture mapping with no perspective correction, per-vertex shading with black fog, nearest-neighbor texels, all of it into a 320×240 buffer. People are lofted, faceted bodies rather than boxes, so "heavy set" and "thin, narrow shoulders" read from across the store, which they have to.

On top of that sits the tape deck: 15-bit color quantization with a 4×4 dither, composite chroma bleed, phosphor ghosting, scanlines, a rolling head-switching band, and the garbage line at the bottom of the frame a real VHS never quite hides. All of it can be switched off in the options if you would rather have the clean PS1 picture.

The sound is Web Audio and nothing else. The chime over the door, the ratchet of the rewinder, the register, the bell inside the telephone, kernels going off one at a time in the popcorn kettle, the vacuum, the music out of that boombox — all synthesized as the game runs.
